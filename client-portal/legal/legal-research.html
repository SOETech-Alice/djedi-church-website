Section 1 Executive Summary

Bottom Line

There is no "501(c)(38)" or "501(c)(83)" in the Internal Revenue Code. These numbers do not exist as tax-exempt categories. The client appears to be conflating several real legal concepts:

  • IRC § 508(c)(1)(A) — which provides that churches are AUTOMATICALLY exempt from the requirement to apply for 501(c)(3) status
  • IRC § 501(c)(3) — the actual tax-exempt category for churches and religious organizations
  • Various "sovereign citizen" or "church autonomy" theories that circulate online but have no basis in federal tax law

What the Law Actually Says

  1. Churches ARE automatically tax-exempt under 501(c)(3) — they simply don't have to apply for recognition (IRC § 508(c)(1)(A))
  2. Churches ARE still subject to federal tax law — they must meet the requirements of 501(c)(3) to maintain exemption
  3. Churches have SPECIAL PROTECTIONS — including limits on IRS audits (IRC § 7611) and First Amendment protections (Hosanna-Tabor v. EEOC, 2012)
  4. "Sovereign" religious organizations are NOT recognized by the IRS, federal courts, or any state as being exempt from federal tax obligations
  5. Churches can be organized as unincorporated associations, nonprofit corporations, corporations sole, or charitable trusts under state law — but ALL are subject to federal tax law

Recommended Path for Djedi Church

Operate as a church under IRC § 501(c)(3) with automatic exemption under § 508(c)(1)(A). This provides:

  • Automatic tax exemption without filing Form 1023
  • Protection from IRS audit under § 7611
  • Tax-deductible contributions for donors
  • First Amendment protections
  • Flexibility in state-level organization (nonprofit corporation, corporate sole, or unincorporated association)

Section 2 IRC Section 508(c)(1)(A) — Automatic Church Tax Exemption

What It Actually Says

IRC § 508 is titled "Special rules with respect to section 501(c)(3) organizations." The full text of § 508(c)(1)(A) provides:

(c) Exceptions
(1) Mandatory exceptions — Subsections (a) and (b) shall not apply to—
(A) churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions or associations of churches

What This Means in Plain English

IRC § 508 has two subsections that normally apply to organizations seeking 501(c)(3) status:

  • § 508(a): Organizations must notify the IRS that they are applying for recognition of 501(c)(3) status
  • § 508(b): Organizations that don't notify the IRS are presumed to be private foundations

§ 508(c)(1)(A) exempts churches from BOTH of these requirements.

This means:

  • Churches do NOT have to file Form 1023 to apply for tax-exempt status
  • Churches are NOT presumed to be private foundations
  • Churches are automatically treated as 501(c)(3) organizations from the moment they are organized as churches

Critical Distinction: § 508(c)(1)(A) is NOT a Separate Tax-Exempt Status

This is the most important point for Christopher to understand:

§ 508(c)(1)(A) does NOT create a separate category of tax exemption. It is an exception to the application requirements of § 508(a) and (b). Churches are still 501(c)(3) organizations — they just don't have to apply for recognition.

As the IRS states in Publication 1828:

"Churches that meet the requirements of IRC Section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of tax-exempt status from the IRS."

What § 508(c)(1)(A) Does NOT Do

  • It does NOT make churches "sovereign" entities
  • It does NOT exempt churches from federal tax law
  • It does NOT exempt churches from filing required returns (e.g., Form 990-T for unrelated business income)
  • It does NOT create a special tax status outside of 501(c)(3)
  • It does NOT give churches immunity from all federal obligations

Section 3 IRC Section 501(c)(3) — Standard Tax-Exempt Status

The Statutory Text

IRC § 501(c)(3) provides exemption from federal income tax for:

"Corporations, and any community chest, fund, or foundation, organized and operated exclusively for religious, charitable, scientific, testing for public safety, literary, or educational purposes, or to foster national or international amateur sports competition (but only if no part of its activities involve the provision of athletic facilities or equipment), or for the prevention of cruelty to children or animals, no part of the net earnings of which inures to the benefit of any private shareholder or individual, no substantial part of the activities of which is carrying on propaganda, or otherwise attempting, to influence legislation (except as otherwise provided in subsection (h)), and which does not participate in, or intervene in (including the publishing or distributing of statements), any political campaign on behalf of (or in opposition to) any candidate for public office."

Requirements for 501(c)(3) Status

To qualify as a 501(c)(3) organization, an entity must:

  1. Be organized exclusively for exempt purposes — The organizing document must limit purposes to one or more exempt purposes
  2. Be operated exclusively for exempt purposes — All activities must further exempt purposes
  3. No private inurement — Net earnings may not benefit any private shareholder or individual
  4. No substantial lobbying — No substantial part of activities may attempt to influence legislation
  5. No political campaign intervention — May not participate in political campaigns
  6. No illegal activities — Purposes and activities may not be illegal or violate public policy

Churches Under 501(c)(3)

Churches are specifically listed as qualifying organizations under 501(c)(3). The IRS Publication 1828 states:

"Churches and religious organizations, like many other charitable organizations, qualify for exemption from federal income tax under IRC Section 501(c)(3) and are generally eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions."

Benefits of 501(c)(3) Status

  • Federal income tax exemption
  • State income tax exemption (in most states)
  • Property tax exemption (in most states)
  • Tax-deductible contributions for donors
  • Access to grant funding
  • Postal privileges (bulk mailing rates)
  • Exemption from federal unemployment tax

Section 4 Why Churches DON'T Need to Apply for 501(c)(3) Status

The Automatic Exemption Rule

Under IRC § 508(c)(1)(A), churches are automatically exempt from the requirement to apply for 501(c)(3) recognition. This is confirmed by IRS Publication 1828:

"Churches that meet the requirements of IRC Section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of tax-exempt status from the IRS."

Why Many Churches Apply Anyway

Despite the automatic exemption, many churches voluntarily apply for 501(c)(3) recognition because:

  1. Donor confidence — Contributors want assurance that their donations are tax-deductible
  2. Bank requirements — Banks often require an IRS determination letter to open accounts
  3. Grant eligibility — Many foundations require proof of 501(c)(3) status
  4. Legal clarity — A determination letter provides clear evidence of tax-exempt status
  5. State requirements — Some states require IRS recognition for state tax exemptions

What the IRS Says About Applying

"Although there is no requirement to do so, many churches seek recognition of tax-exempt status from the IRS because this recognition assures church leaders, members and contributors that the church is recognized as exempt and qualifies for related tax benefits. For example, contributors to a church that has been recognized as tax exempt would know that their contributions generally are tax-deductible."

Important: Automatic Exemption ≠ No Federal Obligations

Even though churches don't have to apply for 501(c)(3) status, they must still:

  • Meet the requirements of 501(c)(3) to maintain exemption
  • File required tax returns (e.g., Form 990-T for unrelated business income)
  • Comply with employment tax obligations (FICA, FUTA)
  • Maintain records
  • Not engage in prohibited activities (political campaigns, excessive lobbying)

Section 5 Addressing the "501(c)(38)" and "501(c)(83)" Claims

Does "501(c)(38)" Exist?

NO. There is no IRC § 501(c)(38).

The Internal Revenue Code § 501(c) contains numbered paragraphs from (1) through (29) (as of 2026). The complete list includes:

  • 501(c)(1) — Corporations organized under Act of Congress
  • 501(c)(2) — Title-holding corporations
  • 501(c)(3) — Charitable, religious, educational organizations
  • 501(c)(4) — Social welfare organizations
  • 501(c)(5) — Labor, agricultural, horticultural organizations
  • 501(c)(6) — Business leagues, chambers of commerce
  • 501(c)(7) — Social and recreational clubs
  • 501(c)(8) — Fraternal beneficiary societies
  • 501(c)(9) — Voluntary employees' beneficiary associations
  • 501(c)(10) through 501(c)(29) — Various specialized categories

There is no 501(c)(38) or 501(c)(83). The numbers simply don't exist in the Internal Revenue Code.

Origin of the Misconception

The belief in a "501(c)(38)" or "501(c)(83)" likely stems from:

  1. Confusion with § 508(c)(1)(A) — The "508" number being mistaken for a "501(c)" subsection
  2. Internet misinformation — Various websites promoting "sovereign church" or "church freedom" concepts that misrepresent the law
  3. Conflation of multiple provisions — Mixing up § 501(c)(3), § 508(c)(1)(A), and other provisions
  4. Deliberate misrepresentation — Some promoters of these theories are selling "church incorporation kits" or similar products

Legal Reality

No federal court has ever recognized a "501(c)(38)" or "501(c)(83)" exemption. The IRS does not recognize such provisions. The Internal Revenue Code does not contain such provisions. Any claim to the contrary is misinformation.

Section 6 Sovereign Religious Organization Analysis

The "Sovereign Church" Theory

Some individuals believe that by organizing as a "church" or "religious organization," they can become "sovereign entities" that are:

  • Separate from the federal government
  • Not subject to federal tax law
  • Not required to file federal tax returns
  • Immune from federal regulation

Legal Reality: This Theory Has No Basis in Law

Federal courts have consistently rejected "sovereign church" claims:

  1. Church of Scientology cases — Courts have held that the IRS can determine whether an organization qualifies as a church and whether it is entitled to tax-exempt status. See Church of Scientology of California v. Commissioner, 823 F.2d 1310 (9th Cir. 1987).
  2. First Amendment does not create tax immunity — While the First Amendment protects religious freedom, it does not exempt religious organizations from federal tax law. The Supreme Court has held that the tax power is a legitimate exercise of federal authority even when it affects religious organizations. See Walz v. Tax Commission, 397 U.S. 664 (1970).
  3. IRS can audit churches — While churches have special protections under IRC § 7611, the IRS CAN audit churches. The special rules only limit WHEN and HOW the IRS can initiate an inquiry, not WHETHER it can audit a church.
  4. Churches must still comply with tax law — As the IRS states in Publication 1828:

    "Churches and religious organizations are generally exempt from income tax and receive other favorable treatment under the tax law; however, certain income of a church or religious organization may be subject to tax, such as income from an unrelated business."

What the First Amendment Actually Protects

The First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause and Establishment Clause protect:

  • The right to practice religion without government interference
  • The right to organize religious institutions according to religious doctrine
  • Protection from government establishment of religion
  • Protection from government prohibition of religious practice

The First Amendment does NOT protect:

  • Immunity from federal tax law
  • Immunity from employment law (with narrow exceptions for ministerial employees)
  • Immunity from property law
  • Immunity from contract law
  • Immunity from criminal law

Key Supreme Court Cases

Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church and School v. EEOC, 565 U.S. 171 (2012)

  • Recognized the "ministerial exception" — churches have broad latitude in selecting ministers
  • Did NOT create blanket immunity from all federal law
  • Applied specifically to employment decisions regarding ministers

Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru, 591 U.S. 657 (2020)

  • Extended ministerial exception to teachers at religious schools
  • Did NOT create blanket immunity from all federal law
  • Applied specifically to employees with religious functions

Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal, 546 U.S. 418 (2006)

  • Protected religious use of hoasca (a sacramental tea containing DMT)
  • Applied the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA)
  • Did NOT create blanket immunity from federal law

Locke v. Davey, 540 U.S. 712 (2004)

  • Held that states can exclude funding for devotional theology degrees
  • Recognized that government can regulate religious activities in certain contexts
  • Did NOT create blanket immunity from government regulation

State Religious Organization Statutes

Many states have specific statutes for organizing religious organizations:

  • Nonprofit Corporation — Most common; provides corporate protection
  • Corporation Sole — Available in some states; allows a single person to hold property in an official capacity
  • Unincorporated Association — Simple but provides no liability protection
  • Charitable Trust — For holding property for charitable purposes

None of these state-level structures create federal tax immunity.

Section 7 Church vs. Nonprofit Corporation — Structural Differences

Church (Unincorporated Association)

Advantages:

  • Automatic 501(c)(3) status under § 508(c)(1)(A)
  • No requirement to file Form 1023
  • Special audit protections under § 7611
  • Maximum First Amendment protection
  • Flexibility in governance
  • No state incorporation requirements

Disadvantages:

  • No limited liability protection for members/leaders
  • No perpetual existence (dissolves if members leave)
  • Difficulty opening bank accounts (many banks require documentation)
  • No corporate veil protection
  • May have difficulty entering contracts
  • No state tax exemptions without separate application

Nonprofit Corporation

Advantages:

  • Limited liability protection for directors, officers, and members
  • Perpetual existence
  • Corporate veil protection
  • Easier to open bank accounts
  • Clear governance structure
  • State recognition for tax exemptions
  • Professional credibility

Disadvantages:

  • Must file articles of incorporation with the state
  • Must maintain corporate formalities (meetings, minutes, bylaws)
  • May need to file Form 1023 for IRS recognition (though churches are exempt)
  • State annual report requirements
  • State filing fees
  • More formal governance requirements

Key Difference: Church vs. Religious Organization

The IRS distinguishes between:

  • Churches — Automatic 501(c)(3) status, special audit protections
  • Religious organizations — Must generally apply for 501(c)(3) status (unless gross receipts < $5,000)

As IRS Publication 1828 states:

"Because special tax rules apply to churches, it's important to distinguish churches from other religious organizations. Therefore, when this publication uses the term 'religious organizations,' it isn't referring to churches or integrated auxiliaries. Religious organizations that are not churches typically include nondenominational ministries, interdenominational and ecumenical organizations, and other entities whose principal purpose is the study or advancement of religion."

IRS Definition of "Church"

The IRS does not provide a statutory definition of "church." However, the IRS and courts have identified factors that indicate church status:

  1. Distinct religious history
  2. Distinct religious doctrine
  3. Established places of worship
  4. Regular congregations
  5. Regular religious services
  6. Sunday schools or equivalent for the young
  7. Schools for preparing ministers
  8. Ordained ministers
  9. Membership not associated with any other church or denomination

Section 8 Corporate Sole Structure Analysis

What Is a Corporate Sole?

A corporation sole is a legal entity consisting of a single person (usually a religious leader) who holds property in an official capacity. It allows:

  • The individual to hold property as an official capacity, not personally
  • Continuity of the office despite changes in the individual
  • Protection of church property from personal creditors of the individual

States That Allow Corporate Sole

Not all states recognize corporation sole. States that do include:

  • California (Corp. Code § 9000 et seq.)
  • Arizona (A.R.S. § 10-3001 et seq.)
  • Colorado (C.R.S. § 7-121-101 et seq.)
  • Montana (MCA § 35-5-101 et seq.)
  • Texas (Bus. Org. Code § 301.001 et seq.)
  • And others

Advantages of Corporate Sole

  • Single-person control (no board of directors required)
  • Continuity of office
  • Protection of church property
  • Can hold title to real property
  • Can enter contracts
  • Can sue and be sued
  • May qualify for state tax exemptions

Disadvantages of Corporate Sole

  • Limited to a single person (the religious leader)
  • No limited liability for the individual (the individual IS the corporation)
  • May not provide the same level of protection as a nonprofit corporation
  • Not recognized in all states
  • May not be suitable for organizations with multiple leaders

Section 9 Membership-Based Spiritual Corporation Analysis

What Is a Membership-Based Corporation?

A membership-based corporation (also called a membership organization) is a nonprofit corporation where:

  • Members have voting rights
  • Members may elect directors/officers
  • Members may have certain rights to distributions (if permitted)
  • Governance is defined by bylaws

How This Applies to Djedi Church

A membership-based spiritual corporation could provide:

  • Community governance — Members participate in decision-making
  • Clear legal structure — Articles of incorporation, bylaws, membership agreements
  • Liability protection — Corporate veil protects members and leaders
  • Tax benefits — Can qualify as 501(c)(3) religious organization
  • Continuity — Corporate existence independent of any individual

Key Considerations

  1. Member vs. Donor Distinction — If members pay dues or fees, this may affect the tax treatment of those payments
  2. Inurement Concerns — If members receive benefits, this could raise inurement issues under 501(c)(3)
  3. Governance Structure — Must balance member participation with religious autonomy
  4. State Registration — May need to register as a nonprofit corporation with the state
  5. IRS Classification — If not organized as a church, may need to apply for 501(c)(3) status

501(c)(8) Fraternal Organization Alternative

If Djedi Church operates primarily as a membership organization with fraternal/societal purposes (rather than primarily religious), it might consider 501(c)(8) status:

IRC § 501(c)(8) — "Fraternal beneficiary societies and associations operating under the lodge system or for the exclusive benefit of the members of a particular branch of the Masons or Odd Fellows or both, or a society (a) which is organized in the United States, (b) any part of whose net earnings inures to the benefit of any member or member's beneficiary, and (c) whose activities are not conducted for profit."

Key Requirements:

  • Organized under the "lodge system" with local units
  • Operates under a ritual form
  • Has membership dues
  • Provides benefits to members and beneficiaries

Advantages:

  • Can provide benefits to members (unlike 501(c)(3))
  • Tax-deductible dues
  • Less restrictive than 501(c)(3)

Disadvantages:

  • Must meet specific organizational requirements
  • Cannot serve the general public
  • Dues may not be fully tax-deductible if benefits are provided
  • Less flexibility than 501(c)(3)

Section 10 IRC Section 501(c)(8) — Fraternal Organizations

Statutory Text

IRC § 501(c)(8): "Fraternal beneficiary societies and associations operating under the lodge system or for the exclusive benefit of the members of a particular branch of the Masons or Odd Fellows or both, or a society (a) which is organized in the United States, (b) any part of whose net earnings inures to the benefit of any member or member's beneficiary, and (c) whose activities are not conducted for profit."

Requirements

  1. Organized in the United States
  2. Operated under the lodge system — Has local chapters/units
  3. Operates under a ritual form — Ceremonial elements
  4. Provides benefits to members — Life insurance, health benefits, etc.
  5. Not operated for profit

Is 501(c)(8) Appropriate for Djedi Church?

Probably not, unless:

  • Djedi Church is organized primarily as a fraternal/societal organization
  • It operates under a lodge system with local chapters
  • It provides specific benefits to members (insurance, etc.)
  • Its primary purpose is member benefit, not public religious services

If Djedi Church is primarily a religious organization providing public worship and spiritual services, 501(c)(3) is more appropriate.

Section 12 Recommendation for Christopher Carron

Primary Recommendation: Nonprofit Corporation with Church Status

Recommended Structure: Djedi Church should organize as a nonprofit religious corporation under state law, operating as a church for federal tax purposes under IRC § 501(c)(3) with automatic exemption under § 508(c)(1)(A).

Why This Structure

  1. Automatic 501(c)(3) status — No need to file Form 1023
  2. Special audit protections — IRC § 7611 limits IRS authority
  3. Limited liability — Protects leaders and members from personal liability
  4. Professional credibility — Formal corporate structure
  5. Banking access — Corporate documents satisfy bank requirements
  6. State tax exemptions — Can apply during incorporation
  7. Donor confidence — Formal structure assures donors
  8. Perpetual existence — Organization continues regardless of individual leaders

Implementation Steps

  1. Choose state of incorporation — Consider states with favorable religious corporation statutes
  2. Draft articles of incorporation — Include religious purposes language
  3. Draft bylaws — Define governance structure
  4. File with Secretary of State — Pay filing fees
  5. Obtain EIN — Apply for Employer Identification Number
  6. Open bank account — Use corporate documents
  7. Consider voluntary Form 1023 — Not required but recommended for donor confidence
  8. Establish internal governance — Board meetings, minutes, financial records
  9. Comply with state requirements — Annual reports, registered agent
  10. Maintain tax compliance — File required returns, maintain records

Addressing the "Sovereign" Question

Christopher should understand that:

  • No entity is "sovereign" from federal tax law — The federal government has constitutional authority to tax
  • Churches have SPECIAL PROTECTIONS, not immunity — § 508(c)(1)(A) and § 7611 provide protections, not blanket immunity
  • The First Amendment protects religious freedom, not tax avoidance — Courts have consistently held that religious organizations must comply with tax law
  • "Sovereign church" theories are misinformation — No federal court has recognized such theories

What Djedi Church CAN Do

  • Operate as a church with automatic 501(c)(3) status
  • Enjoy special audit protections under § 7611
  • Receive tax-deductible contributions
  • Organize according to religious doctrine and governance
  • Make religious decisions without government interference (within limits)
  • Claim ministerial exception for employment decisions (within limits)

What Djedi Church CANNOT Do

  • Claim to be "sovereign" from federal tax law
  • Refuse to file required tax returns
  • Engage in political campaign activity
  • Excessively lobby
  • Allow private inurement of net earnings
  • Operate for private benefit rather than public religious purpose
  • Claim immunity from all federal law
